Case study · Mobile app

MODU

Furniture assembly, made calmer. A gamified 3D furniture-assembly app for Android and iOS, designed around cognitive accessibility.

My role
Project manager, full-stack developer and front-end lead; sound and music
Team
Six-person MSc team, Trinity College Dublin
Timeline
May – Aug 2026

Coming to Google Play · end of November 2026

Website GitHub

The problem

Flat-pack instructions ask you to hold a lot in your head at once: tiny drawings, dozens of near-identical parts, and no feedback until something is already wrong. For people who are easily overwhelmed, anxious or distracted, that turns a simple task into a stressful one. My MSc research was on reducing cognitive load in 3D interfaces, and MODU is where I put it into practice.

The idea

Turn the instruction booklet into a 3D build you hold in your hands: one step and one part at a time, with a companion who tells you what comes next, and guidance that adapts to how you prefer to be helped.

Accessibility as the core mechanic, not a setting

Four guidance profiles change how the whole experience behaves. An onboarding questionnaire recommends one, and there’s a mirrored layout for left-handed players.

  • ControlFelixKeeps every tool in your hands, with nothing happening unless you ask.
  • VisualLumiShows before it tells, each step demonstrating itself first.
  • MomentumSparkyKeeps you moving with short steps and quick wins.
  • Clear PathPebbleHighlights one step at a time, named precisely, in order.

Help for people who get stuck

The companion steps in only when it’s needed: when someone keeps missing the same part, loses the build off-screen, or goes quiet for a while. Each prompt names one action and the exact button to press, and it never blames the player.

What I did

  • Led the team and the build. I managed a six-person team across design, UX, narrative, 3D and engineering, and built the app’s interface and gameplay in React Native and TypeScript on a Supabase backend.
  • Built a design system. A shared design-token theme and reusable components (buttons, HUD chips, panels, coach cards) keep onboarding, assembly, the catalogue and the room screens consistent, and the product website reuses the same tokens.
  • Created the audio. I made all of the app’s sound effects and music, using ElevenLabs alongside my own production, and wired them in so they support the moment rather than clutter it.
  • Designed for people who get stuck. A recorded companion voice, spoken steps, and gentle prompts when someone has gone idle or keeps missing the same part.

A judgment call

After testers said the tutorial ran long, I cut it from 14–15 steps to 9 across all four modes, and made sure nothing ever speaks or pops over the finished build, so that moment belongs to the player.

Outcome

User testing with five participants (three neurodivergent) showed the design did its job. Completion feedback averaged 4.8 out of 5 and visual instructions 4.6, while self-reported mental demand and feeling overwhelmed stayed low, at 2.6 and 2.4, which was the whole point. The neurodivergent testers rated it 5, higher than a neurotypical tester who would rather watch a video, so the app is strongest for the people it was built for.

We then ran it on six devices over two days at the IDM Showcase in August 2026, where more than 100 people tested it, across every age group. Most people chose to go through the whole experience, and 87% of testers finished all four builds to earn a 3D-printed mascot.

What’s next

MODU is set to launch on Google Play at the end of November 2026. After that, the feature visitors asked for most, scanning your own room to plan a space in it, is next on the list.

Case study · Web app

Atlasphere

Plan trips together, in real time. A group travel-planning web app where friends vote on activities, build a shared itinerary and chat as they go.

My role
Project manager and full-stack developer
Team
Four-person MSc team, Trinity College Dublin
Timeline
Jan – Apr 2026
Live app GitHub

Tech stack

  • React
  • Vite
  • Node.js
  • Express
  • MySQL
  • Socket.io
  • Railway

The problem

Planning a trip with friends usually sprawls across a group chat, a maps tab and three half-finished notes. Decisions get lost, and nobody can see the plan taking shape together. We wanted one place where a group plans in real time and everyone sees the same thing at once.

Starting a trip

A new group trip begins with three quick questions, one per screen: where you’re going, which cities, and for how long. That’s all the app needs to set up the shared calendar and start recommending places.

What I did

I was project manager and a full-stack developer on a four-person team, and built the parts that make it feel live:

  • A drag-and-drop itinerarywith resizable time blocks, synced across everyone in the group over Socket.io, so one person’s change appears instantly for the rest.
  • Group chat and votingso a group can discuss and decide on activities, with the winning picks feeding straight into the itinerary.
  • The accounts and comms layeremail verification, password reset and group invites, plus server-side image resizing for profile and group photos.
  • Place recommendationsfor the chosen city, each with a photo, pulling from external APIs.

A problem that stuck with me

Our host (Railway) blocks outbound SMTP ports, so email verification silently failed in production. I moved the whole mailing system to Brevo’s HTTP API, which fixed it: the kind of debugging and deployment call that only shows up once something is actually live.

Why it matters for my work

Atlasphere was my first full-stack build, and it’s where I learned to take a product from an empty repo to something deployed and genuinely usable, working across front end, back end and real-time sync. It’s the other half of how I work: not just designing an interface, but building the system behind it.
