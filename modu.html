MODU

Furniture assembly, made calmer. A gamified 3D furniture-assembly app for Android and iOS, designed around cognitive accessibility.

The problem

Flat-pack instructions ask you to hold a lot in your head at once: tiny drawings, dozens of near-identical parts, and no feedback until something is already wrong. For people who are easily overwhelmed, anxious or distracted, that turns a simple task into a stressful one. My MSc research was on reducing cognitive load in 3D interfaces, and MODU is where I put it into practice.

The idea

Turn the instruction booklet into a 3D build you hold in your hands: one step and one part at a time, with a companion who tells you what comes next, and guidance that adapts to how you prefer to be helped.

Accessibility as the core mechanic, not a setting

Four guidance profiles change how the whole experience behaves. An onboarding questionnaire recommends one, and there’s a mirrored layout for left-handed players.

  • ControlFelixKeeps every tool in your hands, with nothing happening unless you ask.
  • VisualLumiShows before it tells, each step demonstrating itself first.
  • MomentumSparkyKeeps you moving with short steps and quick wins.
  • Clear PathPebbleHighlights one step at a time, named precisely, in order.

What I did

  • Led the team and the build. I managed a six-person team across design, UX, narrative, 3D and engineering, and built the app’s interface and gameplay in React Native and TypeScript on a Supabase backend.
  • Built a design system. A shared design-token theme and reusable components (buttons, HUD chips, panels, coach cards) keep onboarding, assembly, the catalogue and the room screens consistent, and the product website reuses the same tokens.
  • Created the audio. I made all of the app’s sound effects and music, using ElevenLabs alongside my own production, and wired them in so they support the moment rather than clutter it.
  • Designed for people who get stuck. A recorded companion voice, spoken steps, and gentle prompts when someone has gone idle or keeps missing the same part.

A judgment call

After testers said the tutorial ran long, I cut it from 14–15 steps to 9 across all four modes, and made sure nothing ever speaks or pops over the finished build, so that moment belongs to the player.

Outcome

User testing with five participants (three neurodivergent) showed the design did its job. Completion feedback averaged 4.8 out of 5 and visual instructions 4.6, while self-reported mental demand and feeling overwhelmed stayed low, at 2.6 and 2.4, which was the whole point. The neurodivergent testers rated it 5, higher than a neurotypical tester who would rather watch a video, so the app is strongest for the people it was built for.

We then ran it on six devices over two days at the IDM Showcase in August 2026, to a steady crowd across every age group: most people chose to go through the whole experience, and 13 of 15 finished all four builds to earn a 3D-printed mascot.

  • “I’d use this before buying IKEA furniture.”
  • “IKEA should put this in stores as a QR code.”
  • “You should look at a B2B partnership.”

What’s next

The team is carrying MODU toward a store release, and the feature visitors asked for most, scanning your own room to plan a space in it, is next on the list.
